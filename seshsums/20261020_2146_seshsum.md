## Direction

All of ACT-R is too large a next step. ACT-R 7.31.4 is about 7 MB of Lisp, mostly architecture. Its declarative memory (`core-modules/declarative-memory.lisp`, read from the CMU source zip) is small:

- activation = base level + spreading + partial matching + noise;
- the maximum is retrieved, with failure below a threshold;
- latency lf·e^(−le·A).

The second theory is instead Siegler & Shrager's (1984) distributions-of-associations model of strategy choice. It's the right size, it brings probability into CogLib, and its closed form needs only rational arithmetic. ACT-R's own tutorial (`unit5/siegler-model.lisp`) fits the same children's data by partial matching, with no backup strategy, so ACT-R declarative memory comes next as a *rival theory of the same data*. The fan stage that was first planned was dropped.

## Findings

- Moving strength to the correct answer never raises the error share on retrieval trials, at any criterion. Proved.
- Making a distribution more peaked can *raise* overt strategy use, even averaged over the chapter's 12 criteria. Proved by example: (.38, .31, .31) → (.39, .30, .31) takes 7/12 to 73/120. With a uniform criterion, Python found no such case; the conjecture is monotonicity in majorization.
- The chapter's "nonintuitive prediction" holds exactly: counting-trial errors don't depend on the associations, and retrieval-trial errors don't depend on the backup strategies or the search length.
