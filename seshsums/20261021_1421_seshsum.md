## Technical vs substantive results

Five results predict testable **dissociations**:

1. **Persistence vs accuracy** (Theorems 3, 7). Search length changes the retrieval rate but not retrieval accuracy; the criterion changes both. To test, vary persistence through time pressure or "don't give up" instructions.
2. **Retrieval vs backup availability** (Theorem 8). Retrieval rate and accuracy don't depend on how good or available the backups are. To test, occupy the hands or make counting harder. This tests the chapter's central claim that strategy choice needs no metacognitive governor; an account in which children weigh their options predicts the opposite. It is the most interesting of the five.
3. **Counting errors vs associations** (Theorem 9). Counting-trial accuracy depends only on the sum and the counting process. To test, compare 1 + 4 with 2 + 3.
4. **Training vs retrieval errors** (Theorem 11). Strengthening the correct answer never raises retrieval errors.
5. **Training vs backup use** (the Proposition). Strengthening the correct answer can raise backup use, when a strong wrong competitor (such as 2 + 3 → 4) falls below the criterion. This matches the chapter's simulation, where problems with interfering counting-string associations first retrieved relatively often and later least often (p. 276).

The rest are machinery: Lemmas 1–10 and Theorems 1, 2, 4, 5 and 10. Theorem 6, that overt use is (1 − R)ⁿ, sits in between: it isn't testable alone, because R and n aren't observed.

The independence results are only as strong as these simplifications:

- the criterion and search length don't adapt;
- searches are independent draws;
- elaboration is correct.

A failed test would show which one is wrong.

## Clarifications from discussion

- "Overt strategy use" means **backup** use: fingers and counting fingers. Counting from an internal representation is covert and not modelled.
- The peakedness claim (p. 245) is about the **frequency** of backup use, not its accuracy. The chapter says peaking reduces it. That holds typically: a Python check under a uniform criterion found no exceptions, which fits retrieval rising over development. It is **not universal**: it fails when a wrong answer falls below the criterion.
- The document's phrase "the corresponding claim ... is false in general" misreads as "peaking raises backup use". The proposed rewording, for both copies once approved, is: "moving strength to the correct answer usually reduces backup strategy use, as the chapter says (p. 245), but not always: it can raise it when a wrong answer falls below the criterion."
- In the 1984 model, backup **execution** doesn't improve with use: skip and double-count rates are fixed (p. 273, item 5). Using backups improves later *retrieval*, because every stated answer is strengthened, a correct one twice as much (item 6). The Lean model has no learning yet.
