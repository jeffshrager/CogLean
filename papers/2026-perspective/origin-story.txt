The intellectual history of the CogLean project.
by Jeff Shrager
Nov 14, 2026

For the past few years my pastime has been to reanimate early AIs,
that is, to make the original code run -- usually on emultor stacks,
but using the original code as closely as possible. I call this
"Executable Archaeology". We recently reanimated the original
ELIZA. (You can well imagine, given the recent chatbot frenzy, that I
got my "15 minutes of fame" from that!  Our work actually made it into
the NY Review of Book in a review by, of all people, James Gleick!)

So I was pushing this effort back to even earlier AIs, in particular,
of course, the obvious ones are those of Simon and Newell,
esp. because I was a Simon student, and attended the research group
meetings of Anderson and Newell and Bob Siegler as well. (These will
all end up being relevant.)

Although I was interested in everything, my primary research interest,
adopted, of course, from Simon who was interested in this when I was
his student, was scientific reasoning. In fact, one of the reasons
that I attended Anderson's and Newell's research group meetings was
less that I was so interested in their particular theories of
cognition (although that too), but I was actually more interested in
how these groups thought -- how did they reason about cogntive models?
And (having been a computer scienitst as an undergrad), the specific
way that the CMU crowd thought about cognitive models went
hand-in-hand with the computational theory of the main (which I won't
critique here, but will turn out not to matter one way or the other in
what followed), and even more so, the idea of using computer programs
to model and validate cognitive theories.

As an undergrad computer scientist in the mid 1970s I was very
interested in AI -- did AI-related projects for my senior thesis and
masters at Penn, but was also very interested in psychology --
thinking the is we can understand cognition we can build better
AIs. This soft line between AI and cognitive science was nowhere more
central than CMU where Simon and Newell had built their fame (and
eventuall their Turing awards and Nobels!) on building computer models
of thought, and Newell in particular also made AIs. (Simon generally
stuck more strictly to the psychological modelsing spin, but Newell
was the consumate scientist engineer -- although interestingly he
calls himself a psychologist in the McCorduck interviews. In anycase,
that was the millieux at CMU, and it was the one I wanted to be
immersed in, and was.

Fast forward to 2021 when I became involved with a mottly band of
literary critics who work on code as if it was a form of literature. I
fell in with these folks largley because of my ELIZAGen work, and the
story of my finding the origianl ELIZA in Weizenbaum's archives our
"Team ELIZA" eventually publishing a both with MIT Press ("Inventing
ELIZA") is a completely separate history. The most interesting part of
this was actually spearhead by Dave Berry who found Rupert Lane, who
is an engineer and afficionado of early operating systems. Lane (along
with Anthony Hay, an expert on the detailed ELIZA algorithm, and Art
Schwarz, and expert on SLIP, the underlying list processing package
that made ELIZA work), brought up the actual origianl ELIZA, from
almost entirely the actual original code that I had found, on an
emulator stack of an IBM 7094 with MIT's actual CTSS OS runnin on
it. For an old computer scientist and hacker, this was just the
coolest thing ever -- not just "an" ELIZA, but "THE" ELIZA running
again! We called this process "reanimation", and the larger project
"Executable Archaeology".

When the ELIZA project ended I set of to dig up and reanimate the
original code of my mentors, Simon and Newell. This wasn't just
because I had stidied with them; these were the very earliest AIs
and(or) cognitive models, the first of these being The Logic Theorist
(LT). There's a long and interesting, but mostly tengential, story
behind the reanimation of LT, but for the moment it suffices that I
got there in 2025.

Then I start working forward through Simon and Newell's and their
students' IPL-V cognitive models. This was becoming easier and easier
because LLM-based coders were getting more and more performant, and my
code based up IPL-V machinery and AI skills was pretty well
developed. Eventually, by about early 2026, I was able to just feed
found code to Claude, and it would make it run! My goal was GPS, and I
was working through Simon and Newell's archives at CMU trying to find
a complete working copy of GPS -- again, this is a long separate story.

Now, enter Peter Pirolli and Chris bates of the Inst. for Human and
Machine Cognition. Pete was actually my office mate for my whole
graduate time at CMU, and he (and Matthew Lewis, my other office mate)
were members of John Anderson's ACT research group, which I tagged
along with them to, as mentioned above. Pete new, of course, that my
core thing was scientific reasoning, so when he and another IMHC
scienist, Chris Bates we thinking about modeling some aspect of
science or another (it's not important specifically which), Pete
thought of me, and I met with them, or sometimes just Chris,
occassioanlly, talking about how to model the cognitvie sciecnec of
science (a research problem ongoing since I was at CMU with Simon in
the 1980s!)

Now, at this exact time what I all the "mathpocalypse" took place:
OpenAI, using their LLMs and Lean (elegant if obscure functional
programming langauge that has the additional feature of having a
built-in theorem prover) revolutionized -- some day destroyed! -- the
whole matheamtical community by announcing putative proofs of hundreds
of there-to-fore unsolved mathematical conjectures.

Note that OpenAI accounced the "proofs" or "mathocalypse" release) on
October 6, 2026.

So, -- and here comes the moment of invention, which you may already
be able to see, becuase it's sort of obvious when I build it up like
this -- So, at some point in Sept. of 2026 I suggest to Chris and Pete
that what we do is to find some sort of way of reprenting the
hypotheses they're working on, and then they (or we, or someone) would
have something to work with. ... or something like that -- I'd have to
go through the email chain in detail in order to work out who
suggested what, but someplace just around the mathpocalypse (I like my
term better!) -- Oct. 18th to be precise -- I wrote an email to Chris
and Pete with the subject like: "Epoophany".

An aside: An epoophany (sometimes epoofany) is a work I invented is a
"brilliant" idea that comes to me in a flash, and which I then share
widely - among respected colleagues, or worse on social media -- and
as soon as I do, I realize that it's incredibly stupid!  So, it's an
epiphany that goes "poof!" :-)

The idea connects my work in executable archaeology with a more
forward-looking effort to formalize theories of cognition. Newell,
Simon, and their colleagues developed IPL (Information Processing
Language) not merely as a programming language, but as a means of
expressing computational theories of human thought. One particularly
inteesting example is Feigenbaum and Simon's EPAM (Elemetary Perceiver
and Memorizer), an early computational theory of human learning and
memory, implemented in IPL. I have the code for EPAM, I just haven't
reanimated it yet.

So here's the core idea: What would it mean to create a Lean-like
formal language specifically for expressing scientific theories
cognitinon, more specifically, of human memory? (By Lean-like I mean
something like the language mathemeticians use to represent
assertions, which enables automatic theorem proving.)

The idea would be to develop a language in which cognitive scientists
could specify memory mechanisms, learning processes, representations,
retrieval operations, and other theoretical constructs with sufficient
precision to make their consequences formally analyzable. Lean, the
modern proof-assistant language, provides an intriguing model, but
with an important distinction between formal proofs and empirical
predictions. On the formal side, we could prove mathematical
properties of a theory and establish which conclusions necessarily
follow from its assumptions. On the empirical side, we could derive
predictions about observable human behavior, which would then need to
be tested experimentally. And may have already been tested -- we could
take histroical papers and formalize them and then use their own
results to test them!

This is a little diffierent than proof, of course. A formal proof
establishes what follows from a theory, not whether the theory
accurately describes human cognition. Our language would ideally
support both activities, including explicit connections between
theoretical constructs and experimental measurements, allowing
competing theories to be compared both logically and empirically. EPAM
would be a natural starting point, both as a historically significant
executable theory and as a concrete test case for the new formalism.
The larger ambition is to develop a framework in which cognitive
theories become precise, executable, formally analyzable, and
empirically testable scientific objects. I think this could be an
interesting intersection of the history of AI, formal methods, and
contemporary cognitive science, and potentially a substantial
collaborative research program.

Okay, tell me I'm insane.

Cheers,
'Jeff

That URL still works, and opens the first pass that I know of of

Notice that model I chose: EPAM -- the one I was just working on in
my Executable Achaeology effort!

Recall that Simon and Newell weren't trying to build AIs, they wanted
ot use computer programs to test models of cognition.

So what happened was that my left brain said to my right ... (or is it
the other way around...? I'm a non-inverting lefty .... regardless)
... one side of my brain said to the other: Maybe I should try to
apply the thing that just destroyed math, or to put it more
positively, brought math into the AI age, to Simon and Newell's goal
of testing cognitive theories. So I did.... Or, more precisely, I did
sort of what OpenAI did, I gave the problem to the AI to use the same
language that the math community was using,-- this fairly simple
programming language, called LEAN, and try to actually test models of
cognition.

I happened to be working on reanimating the original EPAM, so I
started there, and it worked spectacularly well. (You can see that in
the sibling directory to the DoA one, if you care.) So, okay, so
what's another well-specified computational model that I'm familiar
with and I can use to extend the range of this thing that I now call
CogLean.

It's important to notice the brilliant (if entirely coincidental!)
meta-circulatory of this: Simon and Newell were writing programs to
formalize and validate cognitive models (and incidentally building AIs
as a sode effect!) ... and the model they were building did
proofs. Indeed, it specifically did proofs from Principia Mathematica
-- THE VERY FOUNDATION OF MATHEMATICS! But Simon and Newell didn't put
2-and-2 together the way I did: Why model when you can reason!
Reasoning is stronger than modeling!

Of course, there was no such thing as comupational reason at that time

I HAD JUST REANIMATED!
