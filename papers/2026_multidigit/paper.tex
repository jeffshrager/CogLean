\begin{abstract}
Cognitive architectures such as ACT-R and Soar have long sought to make cognitive theory cumulative, by providing shared computational mechanisms within which models of different tasks can be built and integrated. What such models predict, however, is usually established by running them at chosen parameter settings, and a result established for one model is rarely available, as a result, to another. CogLean pursues a complementary objective: accumulating formally proved theoretical results about cognitive mechanisms. We describe CogLean, a library of cognitive theory in the Lean proof assistant, organized on the model of Mathlib, the community library of formalized mathematics. CogLean is not a new theory of cognition. Its shared library, CogLib, holds representations, mechanisms, theory-neutral statements of phenomena, and machine-checked theorems relating them. Existing theories are expressed as choices of those mechanisms.

We demonstrate a way of doing cumulative theoretical cognitive science. Two independently formalized theories, EPAM's discrimination-net recognition (Feigenbaum and Simon) and the distributions-of-associations model of strategy choice (Siegler and Shrager), are composed with a general problem solver into an executable model of three-digit addition, and their established theorems are reused to derive consequences of the composition. The results form a hierarchy. At the most general level, a theorem relates recognition, associative retrieval and backup use for any problems and answers: a problem confused with a familiar one exhibits a theory-neutral \emph{confusion-error pattern}, stated separately from any mechanism. The same pattern is then proved for \emph{every} memory that satisfies three stated laws (determinacy, certainty and stability), so that a class of mechanisms inherits it; EPAM's memory has all three laws and ACT-R's declarative memory, proved for every parameter setting, has none. Other results separate what follows from a mechanism regardless of representation from what requires particular representational properties. Concrete settings, with exact values relegated to an appendix, serve as witnesses that the hypotheses can be met and that model families diverge. Formalization alone also corrected one of the original theories: making a distribution of associations more peaked can \emph{increase} overt strategy use, contrary to an informal claim of the original chapter.

The multi-digit model is a demonstration of method, not a theory of arithmetic. We distinguish what has been proved from what remains an aspiration: identifying the weakest assumptions sufficient for a phenomenon, and the conditions under which whole classes of theories agree or diverge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Making cognitive theory cumulative is an old problem, and much of the work on it has been done by cognitive architectures. Newell's complaint that the field asks nature twenty questions without assembling the answers \citep{newell1973} led to his call for unified theories of cognition \citep{newell1990}. Soar \citep{laird1987,laird2012} and ACT-R \citep{anderson2007} answered it with shared computational mechanisms and constraints: a model of a new task built in the architecture reuses the architecture's theory of memory, learning and control, and models of different tasks can be integrated because they share those commitments. Decades of work by Newell, Anderson, Laird, Rosenbloom and many others have made this the principal means by which cognitive theory accumulates, and the Common Model of Cognition \citep{laird2017} extends it to what several architectures share. CogLean is not the first effort in this direction, and it is not a competitor to, or a replacement for, cognitive architectures.

What architectures accumulate, though, is mainly computational models and the commitments they share. What a model predicts is usually established by running it at chosen parameter settings, and a result established for one model, or one task, is rarely available as a \emph{result} to another: it has to be re-established by running the new model. CogLean pursues a complementary objective. It aims to accumulate formally proved theoretical results about cognitive mechanisms: machine-checked theorems that, once established in one context, can be reused deductively in another, and that can be generalized beyond the commitments of any particular architecture. Nothing prevents an architecture from incorporating such a capability, and its longer-term ambition is to provide a mathematical foundation for reasoning about cognitive architectures themselves. Related arguments that computational modeling forces theories to be made explicit \citep{guest2021}, and that psychology needs explanatory theories, not only tests \citep{vanrooij2021}, motivate the same aim.

The difference has two parts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accumulating models versus accumulating proved results.} An architecture lets models of different tasks reuse theoretical commitments. CogLean adds the ambition of reusing \emph{theorems} about the mechanisms: a property proved of a memory, say, is available to every model that uses that memory, without re-running anything.
  \item \textbf{Reasoning across architectural commitments.} ACT-R and Soar each embody particular theories of memory, learning and control. CogLean states phenomena and properties of mechanisms independently of any architecture, so that results can be proved for whole classes of mechanisms that satisfy stated laws. The confident-errors result of Section~\ref{sec:classes} is an example: it holds for every memory that satisfies three laws, and so covers EPAM's discrimination nets but not ACT-R's declarative memory. It also shows the present limits. The laws are proved sufficient, not necessary, and nothing here establishes general equivalence or divergence among architectures.
\end{itemize}

Mathematics faced a version of the same problem and has made progress on it. Mathlib \citep{mathlib2020}, the community library of formalized mathematics for the Lean proof assistant \citep{demoura2021}, does not propose new mathematics. It provides one shared formal environment, in which definitions are made once, every result is machine-checked, and later work builds on earlier results by citing them. A theorem about groups, once proved, is available to everyone who uses groups.

This paper asks whether the same organization can work for cognitive theory:

\begin{quote}
\emph{If independently developed cognitive theories are represented in a common formal library, can we derive scientifically meaningful consequences from their composition, consequences that would otherwise require building and investigating a new integrated model from scratch?}
\end{quote}

We describe CogLean \citep{coglean}, an initial library built to test this, and give a concrete, checked answer in one case. The case is three-digit addition, composed from EPAM's discrimination nets \citep{feigenbaum1959,feigenbaum1962}, Siegler and Shrager's distributions-of-associations (DOA) model of strategy choice \citep{siegler1984}, and a problem solver in the tradition of GPS and repair theory \citep{newell1972,brown1980}. Arithmetic is the demonstration, not the objective. We are not proposing a new theory of children's arithmetic. Multi-digit addition is a familiar task, complex enough to need several mechanisms, and two of the needed theories already exist.

The argument is organized around four capabilities, in increasing order of importance.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Formalization.} Existing theories are stated as definitions, postulates and theorems. Each theorem's assumptions are explicit.
  \item \textbf{Composition.} Formalized mechanisms are reused in new contexts, and so are the \emph{theorems} about them. A library of reusable cognitive algorithms would be useful software. A library of reusable \emph{results} is a contribution to theory.
  \item \textbf{Generalization over classes.} Phenomena are stated independently of mechanisms, and theorems are proved for every mechanism that satisfies stated laws, and for every representation with stated properties. A new theory that satisfies the laws inherits the results.
  \item \textbf{Discovery}, in a limited sense. Formalization exposes unstated assumptions, results that hold only under conditions, and conditions under which theories diverge. It can also correct a theory: in Section~\ref{sec:peak}, an informal claim of the DOA chapter turns out not to follow from the model's own postulates.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The aspiration.} The longer-term objective of CogLean is not merely to provide executable implementations of cognitive theories, or even machine-checked proofs about particular implementations. It is to develop a cumulative mathematical theory of cognition, in which the assumptions responsible for psychological phenomena can be isolated, compared and generalized. Rather than asking only what a particular model predicts at a particular parameter setting, we seek to establish which properties of cognitive mechanisms and representations are sufficient (or, where possible, necessary) for classes of behavioral phenomena. Such results would characterize relationships among theories, identify their common commitments, reveal the conditions under which they diverge, and let new theories inherit established results by satisfying previously formalized laws. This paper does not achieve that. It shows that some of the constituent capabilities already work, and Section~\ref{sec:program} separates what is proved from what is aspiration.

The numerical predictions of the example are illustrative. They depend on chosen problems, learning histories, encodings, strengths, criteria and backup procedures, and a simulation could produce comparable numbers. Their roles are to show that the composition runs, that the theorems' hypotheses can be met, and that model families can diverge. They are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:setting}.

Lean checks that conclusions follow from premises, not that the premises describe people or that a valid derivation explains anything (Section~\ref{sec:status}). Appendix~\ref{app:robin} follows a hypothetical modeler through the method, from a phenomenon to a theorem and back from a proposed regularity to the assumptions it requires.

\section{CogLean}
\label{sec:coglean}

\subsection{Organization}

CogLean has four layers (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Lean}, the formal language and proof checker. CogLean uses only Lean's core, not Mathlib, and every probability is an exact rational number.
  \item \textbf{CogLib}, the general library. It holds:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item representations: feature codes, finite probability distributions, semantic networks;
    \item memory structures: discrimination nets, an ACT-R-style activation memory;
    \item processes: discrimination learning, sampled retrieval with a confidence criterion, ``recognize, then retrieve, else back up'', and a decision cycle with impasses and repairs;
    \item an interface any memory can implement (a \emph{retriever}: study an item, retrieve a distribution over stored items);
    \item theory-neutral statements of phenomena and laws, such as the serial-position effect, or the law that retrieval of a studied item is certain.
  \end{itemize}
  CogLib imports no theory.
  \item \textbf{Theories}, each a package that states its postulates as choices of CogLib constructs, cited to their sources, and proves its phenomena.
  \item \textbf{Applications}, models of particular tasks assembled from theories and library mechanisms, with the task's own knowledge.
\end{itemize}

The contribution is not a programming language or a new syntax for cognitive models. Any model could be written in Lean as a program. What CogLean contributes is an \emph{organization} of theoretical knowledge: mechanisms defined once, phenomena stated independently of any mechanism, and theorems relating them, all in one place where later work can cite them. CogLean is far from Mathlib's scale or maturity. It is a demonstration that the approach can work, not a finished library.

\subsection{Mechanisms, representations, phenomena, theorems and conditions}

A cognitive model combines kinds of commitment that conventional implementations tend to intertwine. CogLean keeps them apart.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mechanisms and their postulates}: definitions, such as how a discrimination net sorts a code, or how retrieval samples an answer in proportion to its associative strength.
  \item \textbf{Representations}: which features encode a stimulus, and what information those features preserve. A representation is a parameter of the mechanisms, and its properties can be stated separately (``every digit has a feature saying whether it is zero'').
  \item \textbf{Phenomena}: predicates over a task's outcomes (for example, distributions of answers and of the strategy route that produced them), stated without reference to any mechanism. A predicate asserts nothing. It is a target, which some mechanism may or may not be proved to satisfy.
  \item \textbf{Theorems}: proofs that a mechanism, or every mechanism of a class, produces behavior satisfying a predicate.
  \item \textbf{Conditions}: the hypotheses under which a theorem holds. Some are situational (``problem $x$ is confused with problem $q$''), some are empirical premises that remain unexplained (``$q$'s associations are sharp''), and some are well-formedness requirements (``the strengths have positive total'').
\end{enumerate}
A theorem that has a phenomenon among its hypotheses is a relation between phenomena, not a prediction of a model. It becomes a prediction only when some mechanism discharges the hypothesis by proving it. CogLean's catalog of theorems, generated from Lean, marks such conditional results mechanically \citep{coglean}.

Shared definitions make theories comparable at the level of proofs. For example, CogLib proves once that any learner whose learning never disturbs what it already knows reaches a learning criterion (\lean{criterion\_of\_nonInterference}). It separately proves that discrimination-net learning never disturbs prior learning (\lean{netLearner\_nonInterference}). EPAM's learning to criterion then follows in a line, and so will that of any later theory whose learning is non-interfering.

\section{Components, formalized independently}
\label{sec:theories}

\subsection{EPAM: recognition by discrimination}

EPAM \citep{feigenbaum1959,feigenbaum1962} recognizes a stimulus by sorting its code, a vector of features, down a discrimination net of tests to a stored image. When a stimulus is confused with a stored one, learning grows a test at that point, on the first feature in a noticing order where the two differ. We formalize the propositions of Feigenbaum's report, not the original IPL-V program. CogLib proves, for every net, code and noticing order:
\begin{itemize}
  \item recognition depends only on the features tested on the path a code follows;
  \item learning a stimulus never changes the recognition of another (\lean{learn\_preserves});
  \item a confusion agrees with the stored image on every feature tested on its path (\lean{confusion\_agrees\_on\_path});
  \item every net learned from an empty net stores each image once and recognizes each as itself (\lean{learnAll\_clean}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{DOA: distributions of associations}

In the DOA model \citep{siegler1984}, a child adding single digits has associations of varying strength between each problem and possible answers.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item On each trial the child has a confidence criterion $c$ and a search length $n$.
  \item A search samples an answer in proportion to strength, and states it if its strength exceeds $c$, for up to $n$ searches.
  \item If retrieval is exhausted, the child puts up fingers, which adds strength to the answer that many fingers represents, and retrieves once more.
  \item Failing that, the child counts the fingers, with fixed chances of skipping or double-counting an object.
  \item Every stated answer is strengthened, a correct one more than an incorrect one.
\end{enumerate}
Let $R$ be the chance that one search clears the criterion. StrategyChoice proves for every distribution, criterion and search length:
\begin{itemize}
  \item retrieval is used with probability $1-(1-R)^n$ (\lean{SC.retrieval\_prob});
  \item the error rate among retrieval trials is the wrong answers' share of the strength above the criterion, whatever the search length (\lean{SC.retrieval\_error\_share});
  \item neither depends on the backup strategies (\lean{SC.retrieval\_independent\_of\_backup});
  \item the error rate among counting trials does not depend on the associations at all, which is the chapter's ``nonintuitive prediction'' (\lean{SC.counting\_error\_share}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{A discovery from formalization alone}
\label{sec:peak}

The DOA chapter's central idea is that the peakedness of a problem's distribution of associations governs both accuracy and strategy use. It states that ``a peaked distribution results in less frequent use of overt strategies'' \citep[p.~245]{siegler1984}. Formalizing the model showed that half of this follows from the postulates and half does not.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What follows.} Moving strength to the correct answer never raises the error rate on retrieval trials, at any criterion or search length (\lean{SC.peaking\_lowers\_retrieval\_errors}), and neither does learning from a correct answer (\lean{SC.correct\_strengthening\_lowers\_errors}). These are general theorems.
  \item \textbf{What does not.} Peaking can \emph{raise} overt strategy use. Take $1+1$ with answers 2, 3 and 1, and strengths $.38, .31, .31$. Move one hundredth of strength from the wrong answer 3 to the correct answer 2, which is already the strongest: $.39, .30, .31$. With search length 1, averaged over the chapter's own twelve confidence criteria, predicted overt strategy use rises from $7/12$ to $73/120$ (\lean{SC.peaking\_can\_raise\_overt\_use}). The reason is that the weakened wrong answer falls to the criterion $.30$, so it no longer clears it. Searches that used to state it now fail, and the child turns to fingers.
\end{itemize}

The chapter's claim is not wrong in general; it needs a condition the chapter does not state, roughly that no competitor sits near a criterion. Nothing about this required composition or new mechanisms. It came from stating the postulates precisely and asking whether the informal claim followed. It is the clearest case in the library of formalization correcting a theory's description of its own consequences.

\subsection{General problem solving}
\label{sec:ps}

CogLib's problem-solving framework (\code{CogLib/Process/ProblemSolving.lean}) is task-general:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{problem space} has states and operators; each operator has a precondition and an effect, and an effect is a distribution over states, so an operator can include a probabilistic process;
  \item a \emph{decision cycle} runs a procedure that proposes an operator in each state; a proposed operator whose precondition is violated is an \emph{impasse}, which repair heuristics, filtered by critics, resolve \citep{brown1980}.
\end{itemize}
CogLib also implements GPS-style means--ends analysis, with goals, subgoals and backtracking \citep{newell1972}. Neither knows anything about arithmetic.

\section{Composing theories: multi-digit addition}
\label{sec:compose}

\subsection{The composition}

A child adding two three-digit numbers may know the answer to a familiar problem such as $100+100$, or may work it out column by column. The model nests DOA's architecture one level up (Figure~\ref{fig:compose}). It has six parts.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A problem code}: features for each digit of each addend. Agreeing on a digit's features means having the same digit (postulate M1, \code{MultiDigit/Postulates.lean}).
  \item \textbf{Recognition} by an EPAM net that stores familiar problems (M2).
  \item \textbf{Whole-problem retrieval}: DOA sampling from the recognized problem's associations (M3).
  \item \textbf{A column solver}, invoked when nothing is recognized or retrieval is exhausted (M4). It is a problem space with operators to add a column, write a digit, write and carry, and finish, run by a procedure (\code{MultiDigit/Solver.lean}).
  \item \textbf{A DOA single-digit trial} for each column's addition, with retrieval, fingers and counting (\code{MD.doaFact}, wrapping \code{SC.trial}).
  \item \textbf{CogLib's decision cycle}, which sequences the operators and handles carrying, impasses and repairs (\code{CogLib/Process/ProblemSolving.lean}).
\end{enumerate}

In Lean, the composition is three definitions (\code{MultiDigit/Integrated.lean}):
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
def solverBackup (F : Facts) (p : Problem) : Dist Nat :=
  Solver.answers F.fact Solver.full Solver.lenient p
def itrial E N str as cc n F x := trial E N str as cc n (solverBackup F) x
\end{Verbatim}
Here \code{F.fact} is the single-digit DOA trial, \code{Solver.full} the full column procedure, and \code{trial} the recognize-and-retrieve trial from \code{MultiDigit/Postulates.lean}. The integrated model introduces no new mechanism. Its only correct arithmetic knowledge is in the single-digit associations, which are peaked on each digit pair's sum, as in DOA, and in the carry rule.

\subsection{Why the integrated model is stronger evidence}

An earlier version of the example, still in the repository (\code{MultiDigit/Example.lean}), checked the general theorems with a backup that simply returned the correct sum, \code{Dist.pure p.sum}. That suffices to instantiate theorems that hold for \emph{every} backup. But it supplies the answer instead of computing it, so it is not evidence that the mechanisms compose. In the integrated model, nothing is handed the answer. The answer 250 to $150+100$ is assembled by the solver from three DOA trials and a carry rule, and it is never certain. So the composition must work at the level of mechanisms (the parts fit and run together) and at the level of theorems (the parts' results transfer to the whole).

\subsection{The executable backup: arithmetic as general problem solving}

When whole-problem retrieval fails, the integrated model invokes an executable column-addition solver. It is an instance of the general framework of Section~\ref{sec:ps}, not a separate arithmetic program. The arithmetic instance (\code{MultiDigit/Solver.lean}) defines operators to add a column, write its digit, write and carry, and finish. The procedure \code{full} proposes them as the calculation progresses, units first. \code{Solver.answers} runs CogLib's decision cycle, for a bounded number of cycles (eight, enough for three columns), and reads off the written answer. In \code{solverBackup}, \code{F.fact} supplies the single-digit process and \code{Solver.lenient} the repair policy; with the full procedure, no impasse arises.

Each \emph{add} operator performs a DOA trial on its column's digits and returns a distribution of possible digit sums. The framework composes such steps with \code{Dist.bind}, which carries every possible outcome, with its probability, through the operations that follow. Nothing is sampled, and nothing consults the correct answer. The result is a distribution over complete multi-digit answers, errors included.

Means--ends analysis has been instantiated for the same problem space (\code{MultiDigit/SolverGPS.lean}), where it is proved to work units first and to answer every problem exactly when the single-digit facts are exact. The integrated model uses the decision cycle; the GPS results concern goal-directed operator selection.

\subsection{Composition of theorems}

Three kinds of reuse make the composition deductive rather than merely executable.

\paragraph{A bridge.} There are two executable descriptions of column addition. \code{columns} (\code{MultiDigit/Columns.lean}) is a direct implementation of probabilistic column arithmetic. \code{answers} performs the same task on the decision cycle just described. The bridge theorem proves that they produce identical answer distributions, for every problem and every single-digit process:
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
theorem bridge (fact : Nat → Nat → Dist Nat) (p : Problem) :
    answers fact full lenient p = columns fact p
\end{Verbatim}
The equivalence is proved, not established by testing examples. Every theorem proved about \code{columns} therefore holds for the solver; for instance, that a column error is a single-digit error moved to its place when the other columns are exact (\lean{MD.column\_error\_is\_doa\_error}). The bridge equates output distributions, not internal processes. As accounts of what a child does, the two descriptions still differ.

\paragraph{Associativity.} Each arrow in Figure~\ref{fig:compose} is a two-stage draw of finite distributions. CogLib proves that such draws regroup (\lean{Dist.bind\_assoc}), so a theorem about a component holds where the component sits inside the whole.

\paragraph{Well-formedness, inherited.} The general confident-errors theorem needs the backup to have total probability 1. For the integrated model this is proved, not assumed, by chaining results from three packages (\lean{mass\_solverBackup}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item DOA retrieval is proper (CogLib, \lean{mass\_search});
  \item a whole DOA trial is proper (StrategyChoice, \lean{SC.mass\_trial});
  \item the column procedure is proper when its facts are (MultiDigit, \lean{mass\_columns});
  \item the solver equals the column procedure (\lean{Solver.bridge}).
\end{itemize}
A parallel chain proves that every weight is nonnegative, given nonnegative strengths and rates (\lean{nonneg\_search}, \lean{SC.nonneg\_trial}, \lean{nonneg\_solverBackup}, \lean{nonneg\_itrial}). Both are needed: CogLib's distributions are lists of rational weights, and total mass 1 alone does not exclude negative weights. These lemmas contribute no psychology. They are what allows theorems written for an abstract backup to apply to a concrete, computing one, and the integrated trial to be read as a probability distribution.

\section{A theory-neutral phenomenon}
\label{sec:phen}

In DOA alone, a problem's errors and its use of backup strategies come from the same distribution. A flat distribution of associations produces both more retrieval errors and more backup use, so errors and hesitation go together. Recognition can break this tie: a problem confused with a familiar one is retrieved from the familiar problem's distribution, with that problem's backup use and with its answers, which are wrong for the confused problem.

CogLib states the resulting pattern once, as a predicate over answer and strategy-route distributions (\code{CogLib/Phenomena/ConfusionErrors.lean}):
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
def ConfusionErrorPattern trial wholeWrong isWhole isBackup x q : Prop :=
  prob (trial q) (wholeWrong q) = 0 ∧                            -- (i)
  prob (trial x) (wholeWrong x) = prob (trial x) isWhole ∧       -- (ii)
  prob (trial x) isBackup = prob (trial q) isBackup              -- (iii)
\end{Verbatim}
For any problems and outcomes: (i) the familiar $q$ is never answered wrongly by retrieval; (ii) every retrieved answer to $x$ is wrong; (iii) $x$ uses the backup exactly as often as $q$. The definition names no mechanism, and none of the conditions under which mechanisms produce the pattern is part of it. They belong to the theorems. Mechanism-independent is not the same as directly observable, however: (ii) and (iii) refer to the route that produced an answer, whole-problem retrieval or the backup, which an answer alone does not reveal. Applying the predicate to people requires identifying or measuring the route, for instance by latency, verbal report or observed overt work, as the DOA studies classified strategies.

Equal backup use is not low backup use. CogLib therefore states a second, stronger phenomenon, \lean{ConfidentErrors r}: the pattern with backup use at most $r$. The bound is an argument, not a fixed threshold, so the predicate does not build in an arbitrary standard of ``confidence''. Two predicates for one informal idea is the point: the informal notion of a confident error turns out to combine a structural relation, which holds whenever recognition hands one problem another's associations, and a quantitative one, which depends on how well those associations are learned.

\section{General deductions}
\label{sec:results}

The results of this section hold for every encoding, net, strength set, criterion, search length and single-digit process, under the conditions each states. None depends on the illustrative numbers.

\subsection{Confident errors}

The theorem, as stated for the integrated model (lightly abridged):
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
theorem integrated_confident_errors (E : Encoding) (N : Net) (str ...) (F : Facts)
    (hF : F.Proper) (x q : Problem) (hT : ∀ y, total as (str y) ≠ 0)
    (hq : N.select (E.code q) = some (E.code q))      -- q is recognized as itself
    (hx : N.select (E.code x) = some (E.code q))      -- x is confused with q
    (hs : Sharp str cc (E.code q) q)                  -- only q's sum clears the criterion
    (hne : x.sum ≠ q.sum) :
    ConfusionErrorPattern (itrial ...) wholeWrong isWhole isColumns x q
\end{Verbatim}
The confused problem is answered wrongly with the same fluency as the familiar one. Whether that fluency is high is separate: since a net sorts $x$ to $q$ for certain, the rate of backup use on $x$ is $(1-R_q)^n$, where $R_q$ is the chance that one search of $q$'s associations clears the criterion (\lean{integrated\_backup\_rate}), and if $R_q \ge r$ the model has \lean{ConfidentErrors} with bound $(1-r)^n$ (\lean{integrated\_confident\_errors\_le}). With flat associations on $q$ the pattern still holds and the errors are not confident.

\paragraph{What is assumed.} The postulates are the mechanisms: the net, DOA retrieval and the solver. The conditions are of three kinds.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Recognition conditions} (\code{hq}, \code{hx}): $q$ is recognized as itself and $x$ is confused with it. These are properties of a particular net. They are not arbitrary: every net EPAM learns recognizes its images as themselves (\lean{learnAll\_clean}), and the net in our setting, learned by EPAM from $100+100$ and $100+200$, is verified to confuse $150+100$ with $100+100$ (\lean{Example.net\_facts}).
  \item \emph{Sharpness} (\code{hs}): only $q$'s correct sum clears the criterion. This is an empirical premise that the library does not yet derive from learning.
  \item \emph{Well-formedness} (\code{hF}, \code{hT}): strengths have positive totals.
\end{itemize}
No hypothesis mentions confident errors, or any relation between errors and backup use.

\paragraph{What is reused.} The proof is one line. It applies \lean{MD.confident\_errors}, the theorem for an arbitrary backup, with \lean{mass\_solverBackup} for its well-formedness hypotheses. \lean{MD.confident\_errors} is in turn a relabelling of CogLib's \lean{RR.confident\_errors}, which holds for any task with problems, a familiar set and a backup. That theorem combines three earlier lemmas:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a problem recognized as a sharp $q$ gets only $q$'s answer (\lean{confusion\_gives\_neighbours\_answer});
  \item so all its whole-problem answers are wrong when the sums differ (\lean{wrong\_eq\_whole\_of\_sharp});
  \item backup use after certain recognition as $y$ is $(1-R_y)^n$, which depends only on $y$ (\lean{backup\_prob\_certain}).
\end{itemize}
The last lemma is DOA's retrieval-probability result, lifted to a recognized item.

\subsection{Whole-problem errors do not depend on the solver}

Once $x$ is recognized as $q$, the chance of a whole-problem answer with any property is that of retrieval on $q$'s associations, whatever the backup (\lean{whole\_independent\_of\_solver}). This is a general consequence of the composition, at every setting. Its psychological content is a prediction about intervention: improving column skill, for instance by teaching carrying, cannot reduce errors that arise from mistaken recognition. Only learning that changes recognition or associations can.

\subsection{Two sources of error}

Under the same conditions as confident errors, the integrated model distinguishes two sources of error on a confused problem (\lean{errors\_two\_sources}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Whole-route errors} are always the confused neighbour's answer (here 200), whenever that route is taken.
  \item \textbf{Column-route errors} occur with probability equal to the chance that retrieval is exhausted times the solver's own error rate on $x$, which comes entirely from the single-digit DOA trials.
\end{itemize}
This result is \emph{compositional}. Neither recognition nor DOA says it alone, and it would not hold with the old backup, which never erred.

The two sources tend to leave different signatures, but only the first is guaranteed. A whole-route error is always the confused neighbour's sum. A column-route error has a simple signature only when one column errs: if the other columns are exact, the problem is wrong exactly when that column's DOA trial is, and off by $10^j\delta$ when column $j$ errs by $\delta$ (\lean{solver\_error\_is\_doa\_error}). Several columns can err on the same trial, and a column error can propagate through a carry. Errors can even cancel. Exact single-digit facts in every column are sufficient for an exact answer (\lean{solver\_exact\_of\_exact}), but not necessary: for $150+100$, a units result of 10 and a tens result of 4 still give 250 (\lean{compensating\_errors}).

\subsection{One memory for whole problems and column facts}
\label{sec:shared}

In the integrated model, the solver's single-digit facts come from a DOA association table of their own, separate from the memory that recognizes whole problems. A child has one memory. \code{MultiDigit/SharedMemory.lean} removes the separation, again by composition alone. Each column's digit pair $d+e$ is coded in the problems' own code space, as the problem $d+e$. A column's addition is then recognize--retrieve--back up on that pair (\lean{memFact}): the \emph{same} retriever that recognizes whole problems calls up a stored code, and retrieval searches its associations in the same strength table. The memory becomes a single parameter, used at both levels; the solver is unchanged, since it is parametric in its facts.

Three results follow from theorems already in the library:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The separate table is a special case.} If the memory recognizes a digit pair as itself, the column fact \emph{is} the DOA fact on that pair's associations (\lean{memFact\_self}). So when each column's pair is recognized as itself, the shared model is the integrated model (\lean{strial\_reduces}, \lean{strial\_net\_reduces}), and every theorem above holds of it.
  \item \textbf{Confident errors one level down.} A digit pair taken for another, whose associations are sharp, exhibits the confusion-error pattern (\lean{column\_confident\_errors}). The proof applies the general theorem to digit pairs; it needed no new lemma.
  \item \textbf{Noninterference needs a condition.} EPAM's practiced problems are never disturbed by learning, by a theorem that holds the backup fixed (\lean{MD.learning\_preserves\_trial}). With one memory, learning can change the backup itself: a digit pair the net has not learned may be sorted differently after it learns something new. The result transfers when the problem and its three digit pairs were all learned (\lean{shared\_learning\_preserves\_trial}). Without that condition, noninterference for the shared model is not proved.
\end{itemize}

\section{Classes of mechanisms}
\label{sec:classes}

The theorems so far quantify over representations and parameters, but each concerns one kind of memory. The next step is to quantify over memories.

\paragraph{A result for every memory satisfying laws.} \lean{RR.confident\_errors\_of\_laws} proves the confusion-error pattern for \emph{any} memory, given only three laws, stated theory-neutrally in CogLib (\code{Phenomena/RetrievalLaws.lean}) against a common retriever interface:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{determinacy}: retrieval calls up a single item for certain;
  \item \emph{certainty}: a studied item retrieves itself;
  \item \emph{stability}: studying one item does not change another's retrieval of itself.
\end{itemize}
Together with the remaining conditions (a stranger can be taken for a studied item, whose associations are sharp, and the correct answers differ), any memory with the three laws exhibits the pattern. A new theory of memory inherits the result by proving the three laws, without reproving anything about recognition, retrieval or backup. This is a result about a class of models, not about one model. It says that the laws are \emph{sufficient}; it does not say they are necessary, nor that human memory satisfies them.

\paragraph{Which memories are in the class.} CogLean's theory matrix (\code{CogMatrix/}) proves, for each memory family and each law, whether the family has the law at every setting, never has it, or lacks it at some setting. For the two memories here, both cells are proved for every parameter setting:
\begin{itemize}
  \item EPAM's discrimination net has determinacy, certainty and stability (\lean{Matrix.Discrimination.determinate}, \lean{.certainty}, \lean{.stability});
  \item ACT-R's declarative memory \citep{anderson2007}, with base-level activation, partial matching and a retrieval threshold, has none of them (\lean{Matrix.Activation.never\_determinate}, \lean{never\_certainty}, \lean{never\_stability}).
\end{itemize}
So the class-level theorem covers EPAM and never covers ACT-R, and the explanation of the difference is a property of memory, not an observation at one setting.

\paragraph{What failing a law does and does not show.} That the theorem does not apply to ACT-R does not show that ACT-R cannot produce similar behavior, and at the illustrative setting it does produce graded errors of a similar kind. What can be shown is that the exact pattern fails there: with ACT-R's memory the trial does not satisfy \lean{ConfusionErrorPattern} (\lean{setting\_actr\_not\_pattern}), because the confused problem's backup use differs from the familiar problem's. That is a counterexample at one setting, not a theorem about every setting. A graded analogue of the pattern, with its own laws, would be needed to say what memories like ACT-R's do in general.

\section{The role of representation}
\label{sec:repr}

The specific prediction that $150+100$ is confused with $100+100$ depends on the illustrative binary-coded representation, the order in which features are noticed, and the learning history that built the net. The theorems about what such a confusion entails do not. CogLean makes this separation explicit, and the library already distinguishes three kinds of result.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Representation-independent.} The confusion-error pattern, solver independence and the two sources of error hold for every encoding. The representation enters only through the recognition conditions (which problem is confused with which), and those are hypotheses, not built in.
  \item \textbf{Requiring representational properties.} Some consequences need the encoding to preserve particular information, stated as properties of encodings, not as particular encodings (\code{MultiDigit/Features.lean}, \code{FeaturePhenomena.lean}):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Zeros}: each digit has a feature saying whether it is zero. Then a confusion keeps the zeros the net tests (\lean{confusion\_keeps\_zeros}), and a problem confused with a round problem, by a net that tests the relevant zeros, is answered exactly (\lean{round\_confusion\_exact}).
    \item \emph{Magnitude}: each digit has threshold features (``greater than $k$''). Then a confused digit lies between the same tested thresholds as the true one, and its error is bounded by the gap between them (\lean{confusion\_within\_gap}).
    \item \emph{Column facts}: each column has features identifying its single-digit fact. Then a confusion with every column's fact tested costs nothing (\lean{facts\_same\_sum}), and with only the low columns' facts tested the error is a multiple of 100 or of 10 (\lean{facts\_mod\_100}, \lean{facts\_mod\_10}).
  \end{itemize}
  Each holds for every encoding with the property, every net, and every memory that agrees on the features concerned; versions with no hypothesis on the net hold for every net EPAM learns from an empty one (\lean{round\_confusion\_exact\_learned}, \lean{facts\_same\_sum\_learned}).
  \item \textbf{Instance-specific.} Which problems are actually confused, and the resulting probabilities, depend on a particular combination of encoding, noticing order, learning history and strengths. These are the values of Appendix~\ref{app:setting}.
\end{enumerate}
The simulation of Section~\ref{sec:sim} adds a caution about the middle category: in the learning histories it tested, nets rarely tested enough features for these hypotheses to hold. A theorem that requires a representational property says what follows when the property is used; whether learning makes use of it is a separate question.

\section{The scientific status of proofs}
\label{sec:status}

\paragraph{Validity is not empirical adequacy.} A proof establishes that a conclusion follows from its premises. It does not establish that the premises are true of people. Confident errors follow from CogLean's formalization of EPAM and DOA. Whether children make them, and whether they make them for this reason, are empirical questions.

\paragraph{Validity is not explanation either.} For a theorem to be an explanation, its premises must characterize mechanisms independently, and not restate the phenomenon. An earlier version of CogLib's serial-position result illustrates the difference. Its mechanism was a schedule on which item $i$ was learned on trial twice its distance from the nearer end. Proving that this produces a bowed error curve was valid, but the bow was in the definition. The current version derives the order of learning from a rule over any set of anchors: items nearer an anchor are attended first. The same rule then has a second consequence, the isolation (von Restorff) effect, when a distinctive item serves as an anchor (\code{CogLib/Control/Serial.lean}). The result is still close to its premises. But one mechanism now answers to two phenomena, which is a real test.

The repository records an explicit audit of this kind for its headline results (\code{notes/EXPLANATORY\_AUDIT.md}). It sorts each result by how far it lies from its premises:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{definitional}: a restatement of the definitions;
  \item \emph{close}: the pattern is largely in the mechanism;
  \item \emph{derived}: it follows from premises that do not mention it;
  \item \emph{compositional}: it needs two or more mechanisms working together.
\end{itemize}
Not every CogLean theorem is a psychological explanation. Some are properties of algorithms, needed library facts that explain nothing on their own; some are relations between laws; some, such as confident errors and solver independence, are compositional. Making these distinctions visible is part of the method: Lean shows every premise, and the judgment of what the premises contain is written down, in the open.

\section{Checking the formalization}
\label{sec:sim}

Lean guarantees that the theorems follow from the definitions. It does not guarantee that the definitions say what the prose model says. We tested that link with an independent implementation that could not simply copy the first \citep[cf. N-version programming,][]{avizienis1985}.

\paragraph{Method.} The model was written as a prose specification, with no Lean names, theorems or predictions (\code{MultiDigit/sim/SPEC.md}). Points the prose leaves open were listed for the implementer to settle and document. Before any simulator existed, a list of checks was committed (\code{PREREG.md}). Each check named a theorem, the observable statistic, the hypothesis as read from the simulator's logs, and a tolerance: zero violations for exact relations, and frequencies within $4\sigma$ for probabilities. A check whose hypothesis never arose was to be reported as vacuous, not as passed. A fresh AI agent, given only the specification and working in an empty directory, wrote the simulator. An audit of its tool calls showed that it read only files it had written. Lean exported exact trial distributions for four instances, and a harness ran the checks on 30 random fixed instances and 20 random learning instances.

\paragraph{Agreement.} Every check whose hypothesis arose passed, with no failures. 243 outcome frequencies matched Lean's exact distributions. The predictions about routes, confident errors, carrying, column errors, zeros and thresholds, and learning (non-interference, discrimination, then column work) passed on hundreds to hundreds of thousands of tests each. The simulation predates the problem solver. Because the solver is proved equal to the column procedure the simulation implements (\lean{Solver.bridge}), its results carry over.

\paragraph{What the simulation found that the proofs did not.} Three findings remain open in the current library.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hypotheses unrealized in the tested conditions.} Five predictions (near misses within 200 or 20, round problems, and column facts) were vacuous in the simulation: their hypotheses never held in any of the nets EPAM learned across the simulation's random instances. Each needs a path that tests \emph{every} feature of a digit or column, and EPAM grows one test per confusion, so paths in these histories stayed short. This does not show that the hypotheses are unreachable. No impossibility theorem exists, and a long enough history of confusions on one digit could plausibly produce such paths. It shows that the theorems say little about the nets these learning histories produced. Proof alone did not reveal this; counting how often each hypothesis arose did. The distinction is between logical correctness and the model states that learning actually produces.
  \item \textbf{A gap at zero strength.} With associations of total strength 0, sampling divides by zero, and a trial has total probability 0. The theorems avoid the case by assuming positive totals. The simulator, reading the prose, treats it as exhausted retrieval, which is the natural reading.
  \item \textbf{An unstated assumption decides a predicted learning sequence.} The combined model suggests a sequence: a confident error, then, after discrimination, slow column work, then fluent correct retrieval. Whether it occurs depends on the initial associations of a newly discriminated problem, which the prose left open. With none, 40 of 48 discriminated problems followed the sequence. With only the correct answer, the column phase was skipped. With a copy of the neighbour's associations, the error was stated, strengthened and stated again: 7 of 44 problems were trapped, and 35 were still mixed at the end. The blind implementer flagged this dependence from the specification alone.
\end{enumerate}

The simulation does not prove the formalization faithful to the intended theory. It is independent evidence that the formal model behaves as its prose describes, and it found where the formal model says too little. Three standards are involved: logical correctness (Lean), behavior in the states learning produces (simulation), and empirical adequacy (data about people), which neither addresses.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Relation to previous formal work}

CogLean is not the first formalization or mechanical verification of a cognitive theory. Formal specification of cognitive architectures goes back at least to a specification of Soar in Z \citep{milnes1992}. It covered the architecture completely but at a low level, and it could not be executed. Cooper, Fox, Farringdon and Shallice argued that better theories would come from languages designed for specifying cognitive models, and gave one, Sceptic, an executable specification language in which they implemented several models, including Soar \citep{cooper1996}. Stewart and West rebuilt ACT-R in order to separate its core commitments from implementation detail and to explore alternative components \citep{stewart2007}. That anticipates CogLean's interest in interchangeable mechanisms, in an executable setting. Ragni and colleagues proposed a formal framework into which architectures such as ACT-R and Soar can be embedded for comparison \citep{ragni2018}.

For ACT-R in particular, Gall and Frühwirth gave an abstract operational semantics, a translation to Constraint Handling Rules proved sound and complete with respect to it, and a decidable confluence test \citep{gall2018,gall2017confluence}. Langenfeld, Westphal and Podelski translated ACT-R models to timed automata for exhaustive defect analysis \citep{langenfeld2019}, and proved their timed-automaton semantics correct at the level of architectural components \citep{langenfeld2021}. Macklem and Mili proposed specification languages and algorithms for checking cognitive models against requirements \citep{macklem2006}. In the context of the Common Model of Cognition \citep{laird2017}, Ganeriwala and colleagues have proposed enabling formal verification of cognitive models \citep{ganeriwala2025}. They have also compiled Soar models to discrete-time Markov chains for probabilistic model checking in PRISM \citep{ganeriwala2026,kwiatkowska2011}, which addresses stochastic behavior, as CogLean's exact distributions do. Outside architectures, verbal theories have been formalized as logic programs \citep{rohner2021} and as communicating automata \citep{finkel2025}. Fisher and colleagues derived critical tests of ACT-R from its core architectural assumptions, as theorems over a space of model classes \citep{fisher2025}, an aim close to CogLean's separation of what follows from a theory's core.

\paragraph{Model verification and theoretical deduction.} Three approaches to establishing what a cognitive model does should be kept apart.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation} samples executions to estimate behavioral outcomes.
  \item \textbf{Probabilistic model checking}, as in PRISM, analyzes a specified state-transition model exhaustively, often by numerical methods, to establish properties or compute probabilities over its whole state space. This is formal verification, not simulation. It can also treat parameters symbolically, giving a property or probability as a function of them \citep{hahn2011}.
  \item \textbf{Deductive theorem proving}, as in CogLean, establishes results for whole classes of models that satisfy stated theoretical assumptions. They are independent of particular parameter settings and of any finite enumeration of states.
\end{itemize}
The important distinction is therefore not between simulation and formal verification, but between \emph{model verification} and \emph{theoretical deduction}. Model checking primarily verifies properties of a particular formalized system, or of a parameterized family of them. CogLean's principal objects are the theories themselves: reusable representations, mechanisms, assumptions and theorems. Its aim is to establish general consequences of those theories, and to derive further results by composing independently formalized mechanisms. The approaches are complementary, not competitors. A model checker can verify a large, detailed model that would be laborious to reason about deductively. A theorem can state what every such model must do, and why.

CogLean does not introduce formal methods to cognitive science. What is distinctive, relative to this work, is a combination of emphases, few of them entirely new on their own:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Theorems over classes of models}, not properties of one model or one parameterized family. CogLean's theorems quantify over every encoding, net, strength set and backup, and, through laws, over every memory.
  \item \textbf{Phenomena and laws stated theory-neutrally}, so that rival mechanisms can be tested against the same statement.
  \item \textbf{Reusable proofs across independently developed theories.} The previous work formalizes one architecture or model at a time. CogLean's central example composes theorems proved separately for EPAM, DOA and a problem solver into results about their combination.
  \item \textbf{Behavioral consequences derived from composition}, with their premises sorted into postulates, conditions and settings.
\end{itemize}
Whether any single one of these is novel is a matter for a more complete review, and we do not present any of them as unprecedented on its own. What we claim to be new is their combination: a shared theoretical library, reusable machine-checked proofs, composition across independently formalized theories, and deductions over classes of mechanisms.

\subsection{Relationship to cognitive architectures}

CogLean is complementary to cognitive architectures such as Soar and ACT-R, and to efforts such as the Common Model of Cognition \citep{laird2017}; it is not an alternative to them. Architectures accumulate theory as shared mechanisms in which models are built; CogLean tries to accumulate proved results about such mechanisms, including results over classes of them. It offers a possible formal foundation for expressing, composing and reasoning about their constituent mechanisms and architectural commitments, and an architecture could itself carry such machine-checked results. The present example composes mechanisms at a relatively local level, for one task, without committing to a general cognitive architecture. A longer-term objective is to formalize architectural constraints themselves, so that their implications can be proved and alternative architectures compared within a shared framework.

\subsection{The scientific program}
\label{sec:program}

The central message is methodological. Independently formalized mechanisms can be composed, their established properties reused, and new consequences derived without reconstructing every theory from scratch. More fundamentally, results can be expressed over classes of mechanisms and representations, which helps identify which commitments are responsible for a phenomenon.

\paragraph{Already demonstrated, in this paper and library:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item mechanisms formalized independently in a common environment;
  \item general theorems quantified over arbitrary representations and parameter settings;
  \item results transferred through instantiation and proved bridges;
  \item compositional deductions from independently established results;
  \item theory-neutral phenomenon predicates;
  \item results for classes of memory mechanisms satisfying common laws, and proofs of which families satisfy them;
  \item concrete settings at which model families provably differ.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Still largely aspirational:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item systematic derivation of the weakest sufficient assumptions for a phenomenon, which would say which theoretical commitments are actually responsible for it;
  \item necessary-and-sufficient characterizations: the laws of Section~\ref{sec:classes} are shown sufficient, not necessary;
  \item classifications of behaviorally equivalent representations;
  \item general theorems saying precisely when different architectures diverge, rather than counterexamples at settings;
  \item a large, cumulative library connecting many independently developed theories.
\end{itemize}
A target of the program would be a theorem of the form: every recognition-and-retrieval mechanism satisfying properties A, B and C exhibits phenomenon P under conditions D and E, for every representation; better, one that also shows A, B and C cannot be weakened. The confident-errors results have the first form for one phenomenon and three laws. They do not yet have the second.

Universal results remain conditional. A theorem about every memory satisfying particular laws does not establish that human memory satisfies those laws. It moves the empirical question to the laws, where it can be asked once for all the theories that share them.

\subsection{What the approach bought}

The examples above show four kinds of gain, of different weight. Some results are \emph{library facts}, such as the bridge and the well-formedness of the composed distributions: unglamorous, but they are what lets theorems transfer. Some are \emph{compositional deductions}, true at every setting: whole-problem errors do not depend on the solver. Some are \emph{conditional}, and the conditions are where the science is: confident errors need confusion and sharp associations, and the proofs say exactly which premise a failed test would implicate. And some are \emph{corrections}: the DOA chapter's claim about peaking, the theorem hypotheses that learning did not realize, and the unstated assumption behind the predicted learning sequence. None of these is yet an empirical result about people.

Proof and simulation were complementary here, in a way the slogan ``simulation explores, proof settles'' understates. The simulation checked what the proofs are \emph{about}: whether their hypotheses arise, and whether the definitions say what the prose says.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The associative strengths in the worked example are chosen, not derived from experience. Siegler and Shrager's priors came from a counting process. Grounding these in learning histories is open.
  \item The strongest results depend on sharp associations and on particular recognition confusions. Confusions are derived for learned nets; sharpness is not.
  \item Single-digit DOA assumes that a representation with the right number of objects is available for elaboration and counting. As the column process of a multi-digit adder, its fallback to fingers and counting is implausible: by then single-digit facts are retrieved. The fallback should be a parameter of the column process; the general theorems do not depend on it.
  \item ACT-R's memory is represented by its retrieval rule, without retrieval as use: memory changes when an item is studied, not when it is retrieved during a trial. Every ACT-R value here, including Tables~\ref{tab:memories} and~\ref{tab:shared} and the counterexample to the pattern, holds for that version. Full ACT-R, in which each retrieval strengthens what it retrieves, would give different values, and possibly different theorems.
  \item Carrying is error-free in the full procedure, and columns are added independently.
  \item The framework does not establish psychological plausibility. That remains a matter of evidence and judgment.
  \item Scalability is a serious question. CogLean uses exact rational distributions over finite outcome spaces. Continuous representations, large state spaces and neural models will need different techniques, and exact evaluation already required a merged evaluator here.
  \item Three findings of the simulation remain open: hypotheses unrealized in the tested learning histories, the zero-strength gap, and the initial associations of a newly discriminated problem.
  \item CogLib's distributions are rational-weighted lists; the type does not enforce nonnegative weights. Most library theorems are algebraic identities that hold regardless, but reading them as probabilities needs nonnegativity. It is proved here for the integrated model, given nonnegative strengths and rates, but not yet across the whole library.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}

Natural next steps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item derive associative distributions from learning histories, so that sharpness becomes a theorem;
  \item add feedback-driven learning across whole trials, so that the predicted learning sequence can be proved;
  \item restate the vacuous predictions in terms of the features a net actually tests, or prove when learning can realize their hypotheses;
  \item generalize the retriever interface, so that more memories can be swapped in, and so that retrieval can change memory (ACT-R's retrieval as use), with the memory's state carried through the solver;
  \item compose theories in other domains.
\end{itemize}
The library already contains early work on the last of these: conceptual combination, semantic memory and view application, on a shared semantic-network representation.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The question was whether theories represented in a common formal library can be composed to yield meaningful consequences that would otherwise require building a new model from scratch. In one case the answer is yes. EPAM's recognition, DOA's retrieval and strategy choice, and a decision-cycle problem solver, each formalized on its own, compose into a model of three-digit addition with no answer supplied, and its consequences are proved largely by reusing the components' theorems. The more important results are not the model's numbers but the statements above them: a phenomenon defined apart from any mechanism, a theorem relating it to recognition and retrieval for any task and representation, and a theorem establishing it for every memory that satisfies three laws. The multi-digit model is a preliminary demonstration of method, not a proposed theory of arithmetic. The aspiration is a cumulative, machine-checked mathematical science of cognitive theories; one example does not establish that, but it shows that the approach can produce results of the right kind.

\paragraph{Code.} All definitions and theorems are in the CogLean repository \citep{coglean}, \url{https://github.com/jeffshrager/CogLean}. Every theorem is fully proved, with no \code{sorry}, and depends only on Lean's standard axioms.

\section{The illustrative setting and exact values}
\label{app:setting}

\paragraph{The setting.} The encoding codes each digit of each addend in four bits. The net is learned by EPAM from $100+100$ and $100+200$, scanning hundreds digits first, so it tests only the bit where those two problems differ. The whole-problem associations give strength $.90$ to each familiar problem's correct sum and $.01$ to other answers, with criterion $.50$ and search length 1. Every digit pair has the same single-digit associations: $4/5$ on its sum, $3/25$ on the next number in the counting sequence, and $1/100$ on each other answer from 0 to 19. The single-digit criterion is $1/10$, the search length 1, and finger elaboration, skipping and double-counting are each $1/20$. ACT-R's parameters are a mismatch penalty $\mu = 1/2$ over 24 features and a failure weight $\theta = 1/10$, with decay 1 and temperature 1 so that probabilities stay rational (\code{CogLib/Memory/Activation.lean}).

\paragraph{EPAM.} $150+100$ is answered 200 by whole-problem retrieval with probability $45/46$ and reaches the solver with probability $1/46$. The solver alone answers 250 with probability $\approx .656$. Its main errors are 260, 350 and 251, each $\approx .1$: a tens error, a hundreds error (the counting-sequence neighbour of $1+1$) and a units error. So $150+100$ is answered correctly with probability $\approx .0143$ (\lean{setting\_routes}, \lean{setting\_solver\_x}, \lean{setting\_overall\_x}), and the familiar $100+100$ with probability $\approx .993$. The model has \lean{ConfidentErrors} with bound $1/46$ (\lean{setting\_confident\_errors\_bound}).

\paragraph{ACT-R.} With the confusion graded, $150+100$ can retrieve either familiar problem, or fail. It reaches the solver far more often and is answered correctly about a quarter of the time. ACT-R also predicts interference: studying $100+300$ roughly doubles the retrieval errors on the practiced $100+100$ (\lean{act\_interference}), and raises its overall errors from about a third to about three fifths. EPAM's are unchanged (\lean{MD.learning\_preserves\_trial}).

\paragraph{One memory.} The memory first studies $0+0$, $5+0$ and $1+1$, then the two problems. EPAM's net recognizes all five as themselves, and every value is the integrated model's (\lean{setting\_epam\_values}). ACT-R's memory confuses digit pairs: $0+0$ calls up $5+0$ with probability $15/197$. Two things change at once for ACT-R, the memory holds three more items and the solver consults it, and a control column separates them. With the extra items but the separate table, correct answers to $150+100$ rise slightly ($.267$, against $.238$), since more problems reach an accurate solver. Using the memory inside the solver lowers them: the solver's accuracy when invoked falls from $.656$ to $.385$, correct answers to $.157$, and errors on $100+100$ rise to $.386$. Part of ACT-R's loss comes from the fallback to finger counting, which a child doing three-digit addition would not use.

These numbers are exact consequences of the setting. They are \emph{not} fitted to children's data and are not predictions about children. (Computing them needed merged evaluators, proved to be the same distributions as the model, \lean{SC.same\_trialMerged}: written out, counting $k$ objects lists $3^k$ outcomes.)

\section{Robin uses CogLean}
\label{app:robin}

Consider a hypothetical cognitive scientist, Robin, who studies how children learn arithmetic. Robin has built computational models before, and, like most modelers, is used to writing a program that implements proposed mechanisms, running it to see what it does, and explaining the results in a paper. The program specifies the mechanisms; the reasoning about their consequences lives largely in Robin's head, in comments, or in the prose. CogLean offers another way to work. It supports both \emph{programming} a cognitive mechanism and \emph{reasoning formally} about it, and the reasoning can be checked by a computer, kept as theorems, and reused in other models.

Robin works with a chatbot research colleague, Alex. Alex is useful for brainstorming, proposing mechanisms, finding results in the library and suggesting proofs. But Alex's suggestions are not automatically correct. Lean provides the checks, and Robin remains responsible for the scientific judgments.

\paragraph{From an observation to a theorem.} Robin begins with a familiar phenomenon: children sometimes confidently give a wrong answer to an arithmetic problem, apparently retrieving it rather than calculating. A child who has learned $100+100=200$ may take $150+100$ for the familiar problem and confidently answer 200. Robin wants to know whether this follows from recognizable properties of existing mechanisms.

Alex points out that CogLib has a general mechanism for recognizing a stimulus, retrieving an associated response, and invoking a backup process if retrieval fails; EPAM provides one recognition mechanism and DOA one retrieval mechanism. Together they state the phenomenon without mentioning either theory: a confused problem gets wrong answers by whole-problem retrieval, while it uses its backup exactly as often as the familiar problem it is confused with. This is the confusion-error pattern of Section~\ref{sec:phen}. They then look for conditions under which it follows from the mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the familiar problem is recognized as itself;
  \item the unfamiliar problem is recognized as the familiar one;
  \item the familiar problem's associations are sharp, so that its only answer above the criterion is its correct sum;
  \item the two problems have different sums;
  \item the distributions involved are proper.
\end{itemize}
Under these conditions the pattern follows. Every whole-problem answer stated for the unfamiliar problem is the familiar problem's answer, which is wrong; and because both problems consult the same associations, they exhaust retrieval and invoke the backup equally often. This is \lean{integrated\_confident\_errors} (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Robin has not merely shown a particular error by running a simulation: the proof covers every setting that meets the conditions.

The conditions matter. Lean has not established that children have such associations, or that a particular EPAM learning history produces the confusion; those are separate theoretical or empirical questions. Nor does the pattern by itself make backup use rare. That needs a further, quantitative condition, under which the errors are \emph{confident} (\lean{integrated\_confident\_errors\_le}). Still, Robin now has what a simulation would not give: a reusable result naming sufficient conditions for the phenomenon, which applies to new models without re-running the old one.

\paragraph{Working backward: what must a theory be like?} Robin next turns to practice and strategy choice. In DOA, practice strengthens associations between problems and answers; as correct associations grow, retrieval should succeed more often and children should rely less on overt strategies such as counting. Robin proposes a stronger regularity:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Once the correct answer is the strongest association, strengthening it further never raises the probability of resorting to a backup strategy, all else being equal.}
\end{quote}
Robin thinks this plausible, though it remains an empirical question, and one that would be hard to establish directly, since it says what should \emph{never} happen. Alex suggests asking first whether it follows from DOA.

Alex then notices an ambiguity: what does the confidence criterion measure? An answer is drawn with probability proportional to its strength, and stated only if its strength exceeds a criterion $c$. The strength compared with $c$ might be the \emph{raw} strength, or the strength \emph{normalized} by the total over all answers. The question is not idle. The DOA chapter renormalizes strengths after finger elaboration (its postulate A4, which CogLean's \code{elaborate} follows), but its learning postulate (A6) simply adds strength, as CogLean's \code{strengthen} does. So learning followed by renormalization is a natural alternative reading.

Robin and Alex try a small example, the one Section~\ref{sec:peak} used: the correct answer has strength $.38$ and two wrong answers $.31$ each, so the strengths already sum to one. The criterion is $c=.30$, and retrieval makes one attempt before resorting to a backup. All three answers exceed the criterion, under either reading, so the child never uses a backup. Now the child states the correct answer and learns from it, which adds $.05$ to its strength (A6 gives a correct answer twice the increment, here $2 \times .025$).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Raw strengths:} $(.43, .31, .31)$. All three still exceed $.30$, and backup use stays at zero.
  \item \textbf{Renormalized:} dividing by the total, $1.05$, gives about $(.410, .295, .295)$. Only the correct answer now exceeds the criterion. The wrong answers can still be drawn, but they are rejected, and with one attempt backup use rises from zero to $62/105 \approx .59$.
\end{itemize}
The same learning event has opposite consequences for backup use under the two readings. Robin finds the renormalized result surprising: why should improving an already dominant correct association make a child more likely to abandon retrieval? It is not impossible, and children might behave this way in some circumstances. But Robin now has a concrete theoretical question.

\paragraph{Proof, counterexample, and an exposed assumption.} Robin asks whether the raw reading guarantees the regularity. Alex first suggests that strengthening the correct answer can \emph{never} raise backup use. That is too broad. If the correct answer is below the criterion while another answer is above it, a small increase makes the acceptable answer less likely to be drawn without yet making the correct answer acceptable, and backup use goes up. Lean confirms a case: with strengths $.2$ (correct) and $.5$, criterion $.30$ and one attempt, adding $.05$ to the correct answer raises backup use from $2/7$ to $1/3$ (\lean{SC.strengthening\_below\_criterion\_can\_raise\_backup}).

So the condition in Robin's conjecture does real work, and what the proof actually needs is slightly weaker: the correct answer is already above the criterion, or no answer is. If it is above, strengthening it raises the strength above the criterion and the total strength by the same amount, so their ratio, the chance that one attempt succeeds, cannot fall; and retrieval is exhausted with probability $(1-R)^n$, which then cannot rise. If no answer is above, backup use is already certain. If the correct answer is the strongest and some answer is above the criterion, the correct answer is above it too, so dominance implies the condition. Lean proves this for every finite set of answers, every nonnegative strengths, every criterion and every search length:
\begin{itemize}
  \item in CogLib, for any answer and any memory of this kind: adding strength to an answer above the criterion never makes retrieval exhausted more often (\lean{reach\_boost}, \lean{exhausted\_boost});
  \item for DOA's learning rule: learning from a correct answer never raises backup use when that answer is above the criterion (\lean{SC.correct\_strengthening\_never\_raises\_backup}), and in particular when it is the strongest (\lean{SC.dominant\_strengthening\_never\_raises\_backup}).
\end{itemize}
The renormalized reading, defined as an alternative learning rule (\code{SC.strengthenNorm}), admits the counterexample above, and Lean checks it at exactly the values given (\lean{SC.normalized\_strengthening\_can\_raise\_backup}): the strengths sum to one, the correct answer is the strongest, backup use is $0$ before learning and after raw learning, and $62/105$ after renormalized learning.

Robin also notices a difference from Section~\ref{sec:peak}. The peaking counterexample there (\lean{SC.peaking\_can\_raise\_overt\_use}) \emph{moves} strength from a wrong answer to the correct one, while DOA's learning rule \emph{adds} strength to the stated answer. A counterexample built from an arbitrary redistribution does not show that the same change can happen by learning. The new counterexample does arise by learning, but only under the alternative rule.

\paragraph{What has Robin learned?} The proposed regularity has become a way to tell theoretical commitments apart. If backup use really never rises as an already dominant correct association grows, then the renormalized reading of DOA learning, without further restrictions, is inadequate. The raw reading is one way to satisfy the regularity, not the only one: another mechanism might satisfy it too, or the renormalized model might be rescued by restricting its parameters or reachable states. Robin has not shown that children use raw strengths. Robin has found which assumptions produce, or fail to guarantee, a proposed regularity.

This is reasoning \emph{backward}, from a phenomenon toward possible mechanisms. CogLean does not invent the phenomenon or discover the right theory. Robin poses the question; Alex helps generate alternatives; formalization exposes an ambiguity; Lean establishes which conclusions follow from which premises; and evidence must decide which premises describe children. The example also shows why arbitrary states must be kept apart from states reachable by a learning process: a theorem about every strength distribution answers one question, and a theorem about distributions reachable by DOA learning answers another.

Robin's results are now in CogLib and in the DOA theory, as definitions and theorems. A later researcher with a different retrieval model can ask whether its learning rule has the same monotonicity property, or whether another reading of confidence preserves it, without reconstructing the argument from prose or rediscovering it by simulation. Computational modeling has long made theories executable. Theorem proving makes part of our \emph{reasoning about} theories executable and checkable too.

\begin{center}
\emph{The point is not merely to run cognitive models, but to reason about them, and to make that reasoning available to both scientists and computers.}
\end{center}
